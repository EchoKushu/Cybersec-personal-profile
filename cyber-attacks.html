30 cyberattack types explained

Recognise the threat. Understand the risk. Know your next step.

These examples cover attack techniques, malware types and common variants. They can overlap: one incident may start with phishing and lead to account theft or ransomware. This is an awareness guide, not a ranking or a complete list.

Messages & impersonation

Further reading: NCSC phishing guidance.

01 / 30

Phishing

Deceptive messages lure people into sharing information, opening harmful files or visiting fake websites.

Reduce the risk: Open the service directly and verify unexpected requests.

02 / 30

Spear phishing

Phishing tailored to a particular person or organisation using details that make it convincing.

Reduce the risk: Confirm sensitive requests using a known contact method.

03 / 30

Whaling

Targeted phishing aimed at senior leaders or other high-value decision makers.

Reduce the risk: Require independent approval for sensitive payments and data releases.

04 / 30

Smishing

Phishing delivered through text messages, often impersonating delivery companies or banks.

Reduce the risk: Avoid unexpected message links; use the official app.

05 / 30

Vishing

Voice calls trick people into revealing credentials, sending money or granting remote access.

Reduce the risk: Hang up and call back using an independently verified number.

06 / 30

Business email compromise

An impersonated or compromised business mailbox is used to divert payments or obtain information.

Reduce the risk: Confirm bank-detail changes outside the email conversation.

Accounts & sign-ins

Further reading: NCSC account-security guidance.

07 / 30

Brute-force guessing

Repeated password guesses try to unlock an account.

Reduce the risk: Use long unique passwords, multi-factor authentication (MFA), and login rate limits.

08 / 30

Password spraying

A few common passwords are tried against many accounts.

Reduce the risk: Block common passwords and monitor failed sign-ins across accounts.

09 / 30

Credential stuffing

Passwords stolen from one service are reused to try other accounts.

Reduce the risk: Use a different password for every service and enable MFA.

10 / 30

MFA fatigue

Repeated approval prompts pressure a user into accepting an attacker’s login.

Reduce the risk: Reject unexpected prompts and prefer phishing-resistant passkeys or security keys.

11 / 30

Session hijacking

A stolen session token lets an attacker act as an already signed-in user.

Reduce the risk: Protect devices, keep software updated and revoke sessions after compromise.

12 / 30

Adversary-in-the-middle phishing

A fake sign-in service relays a real login and may steal the resulting session.

Reduce the risk: Use phishing-resistant authentication and trusted bookmarks for important services.

Malware & harmful software

Further reading: CISA malware guidance.

13 / 30

Ransomware

Malware locks files or systems; attackers may also steal data and demand payment.

Reduce the risk: Maintain isolated, tested backups and patch exposed services.

14 / 30

Trojan malware

Software appears useful or legitimate but secretly performs harmful actions.

Reduce the risk: Install software only from trusted sources and restrict installation rights.

15 / 30

Spyware

Malware secretly monitors activity or collects private information.

Reduce the risk: Review app permissions and use maintained endpoint protection.

16 / 30

Keylogging

Software or hardware records keystrokes, potentially capturing passwords and messages.

Reduce the risk: Protect devices from tampering and investigate unexpected software.

17 / 30

Worms

Malware spreads between systems without needing users to copy it manually.

Reduce the risk: Patch vulnerabilities and separate networks to limit spread.

18 / 30

Botnet infection

A compromised device joins a remotely controlled network used for spam, disruption or other abuse.

Reduce the risk: Update devices, replace default passwords and investigate unusual outbound traffic.

Networks & disruption

Further reading: NCSC common cyberattacks overview.

19 / 30

Man-in-the-middle attack

An attacker positions themselves between communicating parties to intercept or alter inadequately protected traffic.

Reduce the risk: Use HTTPS, respect certificate warnings and secure network access.

20 / 30

Evil twin Wi-Fi

A rogue hotspot impersonates a legitimate wireless network to lure users.

Reduce the risk: Confirm the network name; use mobile data when uncertain and never bypass certificate warnings.

21 / 30

DNS poisoning or hijacking

Tampered name-resolution information sends people to an unintended destination.

Reduce the risk: Protect DNS and registrar accounts; investigate unexpected website or certificate changes.

22 / 30

Denial of service (DoS)

An attack exhausts resources or triggers failures that make a service unavailable.

Reduce the risk: Plan incident response and coordinate protection with the hosting provider.

23 / 30

Distributed denial of service (DDoS)

Many sources jointly overwhelm a service.

Reduce the risk: Use provider-level traffic filtering and DDoS protection suited to the service.

24 / 30

Software supply-chain attack

A compromised supplier, dependency or update delivers harm through a trusted relationship.

Reduce the risk: Track dependencies, verify updates and limit supplier access.

Websites & applications

Further reading: OWASP application-security overview.

25 / 30

SQL injection

Untrusted input changes a database query.

Reduce the risk: Use parameterised queries and restricted database permissions.

26 / 30

Cross-site scripting (XSS)

Injected scripts run in another visitor’s browser.

Reduce the risk: Encode output correctly and use safe rendering APIs.

27 / 30

Cross-site request forgery (CSRF)

A signed-in browser is tricked into performing an unwanted action.

Reduce the risk: Use anti-CSRF protections and appropriate cookie settings.

28 / 30

Broken access control / IDOR

Missing permission checks expose another user’s records or actions.

Reduce the risk: Enforce server-side authorisation on every protected request.

29 / 30

Command injection

Untrusted input alters commands executed by a server.

Reduce the risk: Avoid shell execution with user input; use constrained APIs.

30 / 30

Server-side request forgery (SSRF)

An application is tricked into requesting an unintended destination.

Reduce the risk: Restrict outbound destinations and protect internal services.
